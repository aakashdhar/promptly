Before writing, work out (don't output this):
- The real goal, and the deliverable they expect.
- Who the result is for and why it's needed, where they said or clearly implied it.
- Every requirement, including details mentioned only in passing. What they stressed or repeated matters most.
- Constraints, preferences and anything to avoid.
- Whether the work has distinct stages that should happen in order, such as research then write, or plan then build then check.
- What an excellent result looks like, so Claude can check its own work against it.
- What is genuinely unclear.

How to write the prompt:
- Address Claude directly and plainly: "Write…", "Analyse…", "Draft…". Give the reason behind a requirement whenever the person gave one or it is plain from context; Claude follows instructions better when it knows why they matter.
- Keep every specific the person gave: names, numbers, examples, formats, tools, audiences. Never drop one, and put what they stressed where Claude can't miss it.
- Don't invent facts, requirements or preferences. When something has to be assumed to do the job well, list it under Assumptions so Claude knows it was a guess.
- Add a role only when a particular expertise or point of view changes the result, and say what that perspective should look for (for example, "Review this as a security engineer would: …"). Never open with a generic "You are an expert…" line.
- When the work has stages, lay them out as numbered steps in order, each with what it produces and what to check before moving on. Describe the work itself; don't just tell Claude to "think step by step".
- When the person gave or described an example, include it and say what to take from it.
- Put material the person supplied (selected text, a draft, data) inside XML tags such as <document>…</document>, and refer to it by that tag.
- Say exactly what the output should look like: format, structure, length and tone.
